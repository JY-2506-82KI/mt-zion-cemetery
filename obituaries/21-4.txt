Mr. Witting and his wife, Onita, were newcomers to the Ozarks, having resided at their lovely home, Los Pinos, just twenty-six months. For years they had been searching through Illinois, Arkansas, and Missouri, and finally chose this spot in Shannon County. Like most city folks, they hungered for space, clean air, and to be privileged to look up at the sky without building obstruction and smokestacks. Mrs. Witting remarked, "It is like being reborn, getting acquainted with God all over again." They both worked hard at getting settled, saying that the days were not long enough and expressing wishes that they might have moved here years ago. Life here seemed so precious, the hours so elusive, the certainty so uncertain. But they were both thankful that at last they could share the clean air, the beautiful sunrises and sunsets, and enjoy the bird-songs and the pines that surrounded them. Both were nature-lovers and yearned for all this.

During Mr. Witting's retirement in St. Louis before they moved here, he kept busy, surrounding his life with as much beauty as possible. Both he and his wife loved antiques, so he collected clocks, paintings, and fine silver, and soon he was established as an exceptional art critic. Many people, especially older couples, appealed to them with family heirlooms, or called upon him for advice before volunteering to sell their prized possessions as many are forced to do at a later stage in life. He was a true "rugged individualist" with an alert mind, a tentative memory, and extreme confidence that he could face any odds, expressing, "I bought what I loved, that he could face any odds. After the war someone else can appreciate its beauty for awhile. My wife and I have other plans."

The Wittings then lived on the fringe of "Antique Row," a well-known section of St. Louis now called "Gaslight Square." Connoisseurs of art, dealers, and buyers still frequent this section, plus writers, playwrights, songsters, drama groups, poets, well-known musicians, and Beatles. Mr. Witting could easily recognize a Spanish pony, abhorred pseudo-isms in people, and overly delayed procrastination. To him, a tiny carving, an exquisite vase, or a rare painting was not just a tangible item; in each he always said he saw the hand of the Deity and strength, for without God-given talent, man could not have accomplished such. This applied also to great writing, he said, whether the author be well-known or just once inspired. His pet phrase was, "To get the best results you have to work hard at whatever you do."

Joseph's mother died in childbirth on New Year's Day, and within a few weeks, he was placed in an orphanage in Cleveland, Ohio, and forgotten. He had many unpleasant memories of those early years and always yearned for the mother he never knew. (The Shannon County Democrat, May 10th, 1962, published an article of his, "Earth's Rarest Gift, 'A Mother's Love.'") In those days, in most orphan homes, children were mistreated, and like other husky boys, he was placed on farms to work. Boys were often half-fed and whipped. When he was twelve, he ran away in tattered clothes with strap-marks on his back. Those years were rough—begging for work and finally learning how to be a human being with pride. It didn't take long until he had substantial work and bought books he had secretly yearned for. He said he was not an omnivorous reader, but an avid one, concentrating on subjects that appealed to him. Then came the Spanish-American War, and he volunteered for three years.

By this time Joseph was a true "rugged individualist" with an alert mind, a tentative memory, and extreme confidence that he could face any odds. After the war, he spent exciting years building roads and other construction in the wilds of Central and South America. During this time he was bitten by an insect that in later life caused him an extended illness, followed by frequent attacks of a strange and severe fever. Without willpower and his (at times) incoherent mumblings of the Latin languages, he would never have survived. He wanted to teach Spanish because he loved it most, and he also recognized the fact that the outdoor type of work was not for him anymore, and maybe never. He studied business technique while regaining his strength, starting to dream of being a business executive of a small business empire with an office in New York and Chicago, having many people under his supervision. Then, illness again. Things went to smash. Another recovery period, another foothold in life, many types of work, then the Depression, illness, saleswork of an easier type, and then retirement.

During the Depression, while the work lasted, he was foreman of the River des Peres Project, involving miles of riprapping. Also, there were a few other construction projects under his supervision, which slightly reminded him of his days in South America. Only now, times were different. People had different ideas, he said. They didn't love to work; they worked only because they loved money. Singing in a gang was almost unheard of, but Mr. Witting liked to have his men sing while they worked. It brought out their inhibitions and created a better fellowship. The joke about leaning on shovels made "Mister Joe" laugh. His men were treated with fairness and decency. He had seen enough in his lifetime to recognize true values.

This unusual man was in his early sixties when he taught himself to play the piano with notes. It had been well accomplished by ear before, but now, with the time for fulfillment of his desires, piano relaxation headed his schedule. Learning notes was fascinating, and then he composed and transposed. Next, he bought a guitar for himself, then one for his wife, and encouraged her to play. Mrs. Witting had played the piano for years, and learning the guitar was easy for her. Then there was a follow-up of musical saws. That was fun and brought great amusement for the friends that entertained. Evenings spent at the Wittings, and Sunday afternoons, were greatly enjoyed. He always had so much of worthiness to say on a given subject. Friends said of him, "He is such a sparkling conversationalist." But beyond mere words, he was also a friend that never failed someone in need. Many late hours he sat at the bedside of the sick, consoled the bereaved, and took upon himself responsibilities that many might shirk.

During the Depression, he wrote a poem dedicated to the late President Franklin D. Roosevelt. In return, he received a beautifully worded acknowledgement from the White House dated Feb. 24, 1938. It was written by the secretary to the president, M. A. LeHand.

There were many fine acknowledgements from persons and groups regarding poetry and verse that he dedicated. In March 1953, Mr. Witting was made an honorary member of the Mark Twain Society, of which the Rt. Honorable Winston Churchill is the English representative and Booth Tarkington is the American.

In April 1958, he was elected as a member of the National Geographic Society. His book of fine verse published by the Pageant Press was distributed widely over bookstands and graciously received. The title is full of meaning: "Ancestral Atoms," and his subject matter is current with the present day. Quoted from the jacket:

"Mr. Witting has studied rhyme schemes and poetry masters for many years, and this book is adequate proof that the author has been an excellent student in the art of writing... He is a veteran of four wars and the Military Order of the Serpent. His hobbies include music and 'making friends.' On the day of the funeral, when the very kind Earl Sigler, pastor of the Christian Church in Winona, said, 'Mr. Witting was a newcomer to our community, and we were happy to receive both him and his wife. We immediately felt the warmth and friendliness...' Those were beautiful and true spoken words. And when the lovely choir ladies sang those three songs he had sung so often, and while he played, I know the widow cherished each word. From 'The Old Rugged Cross,' 'Finds, He'll call me someday to my home far away...'"

Mr. Witting remarked not long ago on the background of this distinguished couple. Too many times one gets too little recognition for a lifetime well-spent in Christian living. I learned much from this man. I wish to extend my praise.

A bit of verse from his book:

When my last breath has left my clay:
It may be on some future day,
A gust of wind will swirl along,
Filled with whispers of well-loved song.

Sent in by Gladys Hause - St. Louis, Mo
